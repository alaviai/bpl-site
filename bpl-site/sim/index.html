How to use this simulator

Run a scenario. Pick a scenario from the dropdown (or set your own parameters — anything you change turns it into "Custom") and press Play. The speed slider sets how many simulated minutes pass per real second; crank it to 40 for long runs. Step advances exactly one event (one arrival or one service completion) — useful for narrating the mechanics slowly at the start of class.

End of run. By default the simulation runs until you pause it. You can instead have it stop automatically after N jobs are completed, after N jobs have arrived, or at a set simulated time — it halts exactly at that point and shows "Run complete." Press Resume to continue past it.

Reset vs. Reset stats. Reset empties the entire line and starts over from nothing. Reset stats keeps every job exactly where it is and clears only the accumulated statistics — averages start fresh from the current moment. This is the key tool for getting clean steady-state numbers:

Recipe: getting stable numbers that match theory
  1. Pick a scenario and set speed to 40.
  2. Set End of run → Stop at simulated time, e.g. T = 5000.
  3. Press Play and let the line fill up for a while (past t ≈ 500). This early stretch is the warm-up: the system starts empty, so early jobs fly through and drag the averages below their long-run values.
  4. Click Reset stats. The warm-up bias is discarded; statistics now measure only the "settled" system.
  5. Let it run to the end. Observed values typically land within a few percent of theory (for CV = 1 the formulas are exact; higher CV needs longer runs because averages settle more slowly).
Shortcut: Run 20 silent replications gives means with 95% confidence intervals instantly, without watching the animation.

Pin this run freezes the current R, T, and WIP in a strip under the tiles — change one thing, run again, and compare against the pinned numbers.

Repeatable randomness makes the same seed reproduce the identical run (applies at Reset) — rehearse the exact demo you will show in class, or give two sections the same run.

WIP limits add a line-wide cap (CONWIP: a new job is released only when one finishes) or per-buffer caps. A full buffer blocks the upstream activity (amber figure); jobs that cannot enter wait outside the first buffer. Kingman theory assumes unlimited buffers, so expect instructive deviations. Note that a WIP cap changes where instability shows up: the line itself stays bounded, but if demand exceeds what the capped line can process — which can happen even below 100% utilization, because tight caps throttle throughput — the backlog outside the line grows without bound and total throughput time still explodes. The headline tiles keep reporting the process (inside the boundary); the “incl. the arrival queue” sub-lines report the queue-inclusive journey, and the checkbox in this panel extends those sub-lines to also count jobs held outside by the caps — the customer’s experience, which diverges under overload. Little’s Law holds at every scope, each with its own WIP and T.

One, two, or three activities. Uncheck the box next to Activity 2 or Activity 3 to remove that step from the process — the activity and its buffer are removed from the map, the remaining shapes re-space themselves, and every calculation (theory table, capacity, charts, replications) uses only the active activities. With just Activity 1 you have the textbook single-server queue, so students can verify the Kingman formula by hand. Toggling resets the run; parameters are kept and restored when you re-check the box.

Process boundary, and the three nested scopes. The dashed outline on the map marks the process being analyzed: the activities and the buffers between them. The input buffer (Buffer 1) and Finished goods always lie outside it. The outline follows the stage as activities and buffers are added or removed, and appears in exported figures (it can be unchecked in the export dialog). Little's Law holds for any boundary you draw (Lesson 3's doctrine), and the simulator reports the nested scopes explicitly, each with its own I, R, and T: the headline T and I tiles report the process — inside the boundary: the clock starts when a job enters Activity 1, and the count covers the activities and interior buffers. Under overload this scope stays finite and bottleneck-regulated, because all the queueing piles up in Buffer 1, outside the process. The "incl. the arrival queue" sub-line adds Buffer 1 — the full arrival-to-finish journey; this queue-inclusive scope is what Kingman/VUT predicts (its theory value sits beside it) and what the per-buffer Tq/Iq analysis describes. The checkbox in the WIP-limits panel extends that sub-line to also count jobs held outside the line by WIP caps — the customer's whole-system view. The Little's-Law panel, the pin, and the replications all report the headline (boundary) scope.

Interior buffers are optional; the entrance buffer is not. Buffer 1 is the arrival queue — jobs that have not yet entered the process wait there, which is why it always exists and always lies outside the process boundary. Activities 2 and 3 each have a ▽ Buffer before this activity checkbox. Removing an interior buffer removes the waiting room in front of that activity: a job finished upstream can be handed over only when the activity is free, so the upstream activity holds its finished job and shows BLOCKED — this is how unbuffered lines couple neighboring activities. Blocking, starving, and inventory build-up relocate accordingly, and Kingman/VUT theory (which assumes unlimited buffers) will instructively deviate — see the note under the theory table. Toggling a buffer resets the run.

Activity states. The map uses the lab exercises' conventions. Each activity carries a small progress dial at its top-right corner — one sweep of the orange arc is one job (red for a longer-than-average job); while blocked the dial sits full. State words appear inside the box: BLOCKED (steady red) when the activity holds a finished job it cannot pass on — the outgoing arrow also turns red with a slash across it, and the held job sinks to the floor of the box in amber; STARVED (blinking blue) when upstream feeds it nothing; lowercase idle (blinking, muted) at Activity 1 when no demand has arrived. Nothing is shown while the activity is simply working — the dial carries that. With the details checkbox on, the accumulated percentage of time in each state appears under the activity — the classic utilization decomposition: busy + starved/idle + blocked = 100%.

Screen space. Every analysis card has a – button to minimize it, so you can pair the process map with just the chart you are teaching from. The Little’s Law chart adds + / − / A y-axis zoom; the waiting-time chart has a selector for each activity or the whole line. The details checkbox on the process map reveals time-in-state percentages, each activity’s capacity, and each buffer’s observed Tq (avg wait) and Iq (avg queue length) — keep it off for a clean map when introducing the layout.

The science: Little’s Law, queueing & variability

Little’s Law — WIP = R × T. The long-run average number of jobs in a system equals throughput times average throughput time. Remarkably, it holds for any stable system — regardless of arrival pattern, service distributions, or queue discipline — which is why it is often called the most general law in operations. Proven formally by Little (1961); see Little (2011) for a 50-year retrospective. In Cachon & Terwiesch notation it reads I = R × T. Note it relates long-run averages: the instantaneous WIP fluctuates constantly (the gray line in the Little’s Law panel) even while the law holds exactly for the averages.

Why queues form — the VUT equation. Kingman’s heavy-traffic approximation decomposes waiting into Variability × Utilization × Time: Tq ≈ [(CVa² + CVp²)/2] · [ρ/(1−ρ)] · p (Kingman 1961). With no variability there is no queue at any utilization up to and including 100% — a deterministic line can balance exactly at capacity (a knife-edge). With any variability, waiting explodes nonlinearly as ρ → 1 (the “hockey stick” panel) and ρ = 100% becomes unsustainable: the queue behaves like an un-drifted random walk and grows without bound. The formula is exact for the M/M/1 queue (CV = 1) and, with the (1+CVp²)/2 factor, for M/G/1 via Pollaczek–Khinchine.

Variability propagates. An activity’s departures inherit variability from both its arrivals and its own processing: CVd² = ρ²CVp² + (1−ρ²)CVa². This “linking equation,” used in Whitt’s Queueing Network Analyzer (1983) and popularized by Factory Physics, explains why one variable step creates queues at downstream activities that are themselves perfectly steady (Scenario 6).

Bottlenecks & capacity. Each activity’s capacity is 1/p; a serial line’s capacity is the minimum — the process can never produce faster than its slowest step. System utilization = R / capacity. Balancing flow to the bottleneck is the heart of the Theory of Constraints (Goldratt & Cox, The Goal).

WIP caps & CONWIP. Capping WIP stabilizes throughput time (by Little’s Law, T = WIP/R when WIP is fixed) at some cost in throughput — the pull logic behind kanban and CONWIP (Spearman, Woodruff & Hopp 1990).

Simulation practice. One animated run is a single random sample; short runs are biased by the empty-system warm-up. Standard remedies — deleting the warm-up (Reset stats) and averaging independent replications with confidence intervals — follow Law’s Simulation Modeling and Analysis.
